================================================================================
WHAT THE CONTRASTIVE BACKBONE REPRESENTS, AND HOW A HELD-OUT ROBOT DIFFERS
tower: outputs/siglip_pretrain/all4_n42_all
tools: analyze_pose_invariance.py, visualize_embodiment_representation.py
written 2026-09-08
================================================================================

THE ONE-LINE ANSWER
  A robot the tower has never seen is represented almost exactly like one it trained
  on, as long as the two are at the same end-effector pose. Being held out costs
  0.006 of cosine similarity. What the tower encodes is the POSE; which robot is in
  the picture is nearly invisible to it.

  That is the property the dual-tower Diffusion Policy experiment is built on, so it
  is measured here rather than assumed -- and the same measurements show exactly
  where it stops working, which matters more for the policy than the good news does.


================================================================================
0. WHAT THE DATA LOOKS LIKE   (figure: outputs/embodiment_grid.png)
================================================================================
  The corpus is 56 robot arms, each posed at the SAME 48 canonical end-effector
  states, rendered against 12 backgrounds from 4 camera views in 3 colour variants.
  There is no task and no object manipulation -- just an arm holding a pose.

  42 of the 56 embodiments were used for pre-training. 14 were held out entirely:
      0, 1, 3, 8, 12, 14, 23, 27, 28, 33, 34, 36, 42, 49

  The grid figure shows four canonical poses (rows) rendered on four TRAINED arms
  (blue borders) and four HELD-OUT arms (red borders). Every image in a row is the
  identical end-effector state. What varies down a row is the arm: link proportions,
  joint layout, gripper geometry, and colour -- a slim white arm next to a thick
  orange-and-grey one next to a KUKA. What does NOT vary is where the gripper is.

  This is the whole premise in one picture: if a model can look at any of those
  eight images and report the same pose, it has separated embodiment from
  configuration.

  IMPORTANT: only about 21% of (embodiment, pose, background, view, colour)
  combinations were actually rendered, so a naive grid comes out mostly blank. The
  figure is built from combinations that exist.


================================================================================
1. SAME POSE, DIFFERENT ROBOT   (all nuisance axes held fixed)
================================================================================
  Background, camera view and colour are held FIXED so the only thing differing
  between two frames is the pose and the robot. Cosines are CENTRED -- see section 5
  for why the raw numbers are useless here.

                                        contrastive tower      stock (control)
                                        centred     raw        centred     raw
    same pose, trained x trained         +0.979    +0.982       +0.116    +0.967
    same pose, trained x HELD-OUT        +0.974    +0.977       +0.104    +0.968
    same pose, HELD-OUT x HELD-OUT       +0.971    +0.975       +0.109    +0.968
    DIFFERENT pose, same robot           -0.042    +0.105       -0.007    +0.963

  Read the first three rows against the fourth. Two robots the tower has NEVER seen,
  at the same pose, sit at +0.971. The SAME robot at a different pose sits at -0.042.
  Identity costs 0.008; pose costs 1.013. The representation is reading configuration
  and discarding morphology, and it does so whether or not it trained on the arm.

  The stock tower separates the same conditions by 0.12 versus -0.01 -- a real but
  tiny effect, and it is what SigLIP already gives you for free. The contrastive
  pre-training is responsible for the rest.


================================================================================
2. PER-EMBODIMENT -- is one robot dragging the average?   (embodiment_representation.json)
================================================================================
  Mean similarity of each embodiment to all OTHER embodiments at the same pose:

                    n      mean      min       max
    trained        41    +0.974   +0.925   +0.986
    HELD-OUT       14    +0.968   +0.937   +0.982

    stock, trained 41    +0.089   -0.063   +0.261
    stock, HELD-OUT 14   +0.096   -0.024   +0.164

  No held-out robot fails. The weakest embodiment overall is #25 at +0.925 -- and it
  is a TRAINED one. The five weakest are #25 (trained), #14 (held-out), #38
  (trained), #12 (held-out), #54 (trained): the two groups are interleaved, which is
  what you want to see. "Held-out works" is not an average hiding a failure.

  The held-out RANGE is actually narrower (0.937-0.982 against 0.925-0.986). Nothing
  should be read into that; it is 14 samples against 41.


================================================================================
3. THE FEATURE SPACE   (figure: outputs/embodiment_pca.png)
================================================================================
  PCA of the centred features. Colour = which of the canonical poses. Circle = a
  trained embodiment, triangle = one never seen.

  CONTRASTIVE TOWER: tight, well-separated clusters, one per pose, and within every
  cluster the circles and triangles are piled on top of each other. A held-out robot
  lands with its POSE, not with other held-out robots. The top two components carry
  57% and 44% of the variance -- the structure is genuinely low-dimensional.

  STOCK SIGLIP: a single uniform smear. Colours are scattered, and trained and
  held-out points are equally scattered. There is no pose structure to find.

  This is the same result as section 1 with nothing averaged away, and it is the
  figure to show someone who does not want to read a table.


================================================================================
4. HOW SHARPLY IS POSE ENCODED?
================================================================================
  Centred similarity against the real distance between two poses:

    pose separation      contrastive      stock
    0-1 cm (same pose)     +0.974         +0.108
    1-5 cm                 +0.239         +0.098
    5-10 cm                +0.034         +0.045
    10-20 cm               -0.074         -0.010
    20-100 cm              -0.137         -0.106

  This is the one result that is not straightforwardly good. The falloff is very
  sharp: poses only 1-5 cm apart drop from +0.974 to +0.239. The tower behaves more
  like a lookup that identifies WHICH of the 48 canonical poses it is seeing than
  like a smooth embedding of pose as a continuous quantity.

  For retrieval that is ideal -- and retrieval is perfect: hand it a held-out robot's
  image and the nearest training frame shares the pose 100% of the time, median EEF
  error 0.0 cm, against 22% and 7.9 cm for stock.

  For a POLICY it is less good. A controller wants nearby poses to have nearby
  features, so that a small error in the visual estimate produces a small error in
  the action. A representation peaked at 48 discrete points offers little to
  interpolate across. The obvious thing to check is the newer 8,000-pair export,
  which has far denser pose coverage.


================================================================================
5. TWO METHODOLOGICAL POINTS THAT CHANGE THE NUMBERS
================================================================================
  RAW COSINES ARE MEANINGLESS HERE. SigLIP features are anisotropic enough that any
  two images sit near raw 0.95. The stock tower proves it: raw +0.967 for the same
  pose and +0.963 for a different one -- a gap of 0.004 that would read as
  "invariant" if you stopped at the raw column. Subtracting the batch mean turns that
  into +0.116 against -0.007. Every headline number in this document is centred.

  THE NUISANCE AXES MUST BE HELD FIXED TO MEASURE WHAT YOU THINK. If background and
  camera view are free to vary, a low similarity could be the backdrop rather than
  the pose or the robot. Section 1 holds them fixed; section 6 relaxes them
  deliberately.


================================================================================
6. WHERE THE INVARIANCE STOPS -- and this is the part that matters for the policy
================================================================================
  Relaxing the nuisance axes one at a time:

    allowed to differ                  trained x HELD-OUT   held-out retrieval top-1
    nothing (pose and robot only)            +0.974                1.000
    + background, robot paint                +0.969                0.993
    + camera view                            +0.951                0.979

  Backdrop and paint cost nothing. Camera view costs 0.023 -- but note WHY it
  survives: all four canonical views are IN the training data. This is invariance
  across geometries it has seen, not invariance to geometry.

  Measured separately, as a fraction of the distance a real pose change produces:

        different background ....  1%      shift the image 40 px .... 31%
        furniture recolour ......  2%      zoom out to 0.75x ........ 41%
        A DIFFERENT ROBOT .......  4%      a real RoboCasa frame .... 102%

  A 40-pixel camera shift moves the feature EIGHT TIMES further than swapping the
  robot does. And a real RoboCasa policy frame sits at 102% -- as far from a
  synthetic frame as a completely unrelated image. Fully decorrelated.

  The cause is structural, not a bug: the tower trained on four fixed camera poses,
  which put the gripper in tight 12-22 px clusters. A fifth geometry is out of
  distribution and the representation has nothing to say about it.


================================================================================
7. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE DIFFUSION POLICY EXPERIMENTS
================================================================================
  SUPPORTS: the premise. On robots it never saw, the tower encodes EEF pose and
  ignores identity. If a policy can read that, it gets embodiment-invariant pose
  information without a single action demonstration on those robots.

  EXPLAINS THE FAILURE ALREADY OBSERVED: a single frozen contrastive tower gave the
  policy exactly nothing -- 0.0388 against a stock tower's 0.0387 (CLAUDE.md 9.6).
  Section 6 is why. The representation is excellent on the domain it was trained on
  and empty on the one the policy actually sees.

  WHAT THE DUAL-TOWER RUNS TEST: putting a trainable stock tower alongside the frozen
  contrastive one, so the trainable half can supply what the policy needs while the
  frozen half adds embodiment-invariant structure on top. It can then only help or be
  ignored. Given section 6, expect modest at best.

  THE RUN THAT SETTLES IT: D_freeze_c_drop, which drops the frozen branch 30% of the
  time. If that costs nothing, the policy was never reading it.

8. THE SAME QUESTION ON THE REAL JACO   (tools/analyze_jaco_representation.py)
================================================================================
  Everything above is synthetic. The obvious follow-up is whether it holds on real
  robot images, and the answer turns out to be YES -- but only once a confound is
  removed, and the confound reverses the naive reading completely.

  SETUP. visual_robust_new_barx_ur5e renders the SAME 108 episodes from six NAMED
  embodiments, and observation.state is shared across all six. So one row is one EEF
  state seen on six different arms, which is exactly the comparison. 48 states,
  4 per episode, 288 frames.
      non-Jaco: PandaOmron, IIWAOmron, UR5eOmron, PandaOmronPandaGripper
      Jaco:     JacoOmron, JacoOmronPandaGripper

8.1 THE HEADLINE NUMBERS, AND WHY THEY MISLEAD ON THEIR OWN
                                        contrastive       stock
    same state, non-Jaco x non-Jaco       +0.501         +0.525
    same state, non-Jaco x JACO           +0.492         +0.480
    same state, JACO x JACO               +0.485         +0.765
    DIFFERENT state, same embodiment      +0.018         +0.060
    Jaco -> nearest non-Jaco, top-1        0.406          0.729
    median EEF error                      19.7 cm         0.0 cm

  Read alone, this says the STOCK tower is better on real images -- 0.729 retrieval
  against 0.406. That reading is wrong.

8.2 THE CONFOUND, AND THE CONTROL THAT EXPOSES IT
  Two renders of the same row share the ENTIRE scene: same kitchen, same object
  positions, same lighting, same instant. Only the arm differs. A tower that matches
  on SCENE rather than on pose scores perfectly on that without ever looking at the
  robot.

  The control is pairs from the SAME episode at DIFFERENT times. The scene is nearly
  identical and only the arm has moved -- a comparison background cannot win.

                                same state   same scene,      DIFFERENCE
                                             different pose   (= pose discrimination)
    contrastive                   +0.495        +0.004           +0.491
    stock SigLIP                  +0.517        +0.296           +0.221

  There it is. The contrastive tower scores +0.004 when the scene is held and only
  the arm moves -- it gets essentially NOTHING from the background, so its +0.495 is
  entirely about the end-effector state. Stock scores +0.296 on the same control, so
  more than half of its apparent similarity is the kitchen.

  On pose discrimination proper, the contrastive tower beats stock by 0.491 to 0.221
  -- better than 2x. Stock's higher retrieval number is a shortcut: it recognises
  WHICH MOMENT of the episode it is looking at, which happens to identify the row,
  and would not survive a change of scene.

8.3 AND JACO SPECIFICALLY
    contrastive:  non-Jaco x non-Jaco +0.501   vs   non-Jaco x JACO +0.492
                  -> being Jaco costs 0.009
    stock:        +0.525 vs +0.480  -> costs 0.045

  0.009 is the same price held-out embodiments paid in the synthetic test (section
  1: +0.979 vs +0.974, a cost of 0.005). The property transfers to a real arm.

8.4 HOW THIS SQUARES WITH THE 102% IN SECTION 6
  Section 6 said a real RoboCasa frame sits 102% of the way from a synthetic frame
  to an unrelated image, which sounds like it should rule this out. It does not, and
  the distinction is worth being precise about:

    * ACROSS domains the features are far apart. A real frame does not land near
      any synthetic frame. That is the 102%.
    * WITHIN the real domain the features still order themselves by EEF state. That
      is section 8.

  Both are true. The tower has not learned a shared synthetic/real coordinate
  system, but the structure it learned -- separate pose, ignore appearance -- still
  operates on real images. For the dual-tower policy experiment that is better news
  than section 6 alone suggested: the frozen tower is not noise on policy frames, it
  is a pose-sensitive, scene-insensitive signal in a part of the space the policy's
  own encoder does not occupy.

  It does NOT explain away the 9.6 result (frozen 0.0388 vs stock 0.0387). Whether
  the policy can USE this signal is still open, and D_freeze_c_drop is still the run
  that settles it.
